Life support systems aboard the ISS supply air, water, and temperature regulation. The Environmental Control and Life Support System (ECLSS) includes systems for oxygen generation, carbon dioxide removal, humidity control, and water recycling. For example, the Oxygen Generation System (OGS) can electrolyze water to produce oxygen for breathing, while the Water Recovery System (WRS) recovers water from urine, humidity condensate, and other wastewater streams to recycle it as potable water. Crew use compact exercise machines such as the Advanced Resistive Exercise Device (ARED) and the T2 treadmill to mitigate muscle and bone loss under microgravity.

Scientific research aboard the ISS spans many disciplines. In biology and biotechnology, experiments investigate the effects of microgravity on cell cultures, plants, and small animals to better understand fundamental physiological processes. In human research, long-duration experiments examine the effects of spaceflight on bone density, muscle atrophy, cardiovascular function, vision changes, and fluid shifts. In physical sciences, facilities such as the Cold Atom Laboratory, the Materials Science Laboratory, and the Microgravity Science Glovebox enable experiments in fluid physics, combustion, materials processing, and fundamental physics.

The ISS also hosts Earth observation instruments and remote sensing payloads, such as high-resolution cameras, radar, and atmospheric sensors, which contribute to climate monitoring, disaster response, and environmental science. Some modules and external platforms carry exposed experiments for testing materials in the space environment, radiation detectors, and space weather instruments.

Crew rotations generally occur about every six months, using spacecraft such as the Crew Dragon or Soyuz to ferry astronauts, supplies, and experiments. Resupply missions deliver food, water, spare parts, experiments, and other logistics using vehicles like SpaceX’s Cargo Dragon, Russia’s Progress, Northrop Grumman’s Cygnus, and formerly the European ATV and Japanese HTV vehicles.

Over its lifetime, the ISS has undergone upgrades and maintenance. Aging hardware has been replaced or supplemented, and new modules or external platforms added. The station’s structure must contend with orbital debris, micrometeoroids, and radiation. Shielding, protective Whipple bumpers, and careful monitoring help reduce risks. Operations involve extensive ground support from mission control centers in Houston, Moscow, Cologne, and Tsukuba, coordinating maintainance, experiments, scheduling, and safety protocols.

The future of the ISS is being discussed among the international partners. Planned operations currently extend to at least 2028 or potentially 2030. After that period, proposals exist for commercial successors, modular free-flyers, or national space stations operated by individual countries or private companies. Deorbiting the ISS will require a controlled reentry to ensure debris does not endanger populated areas; the final phase could involve gradually lowering altitude or breaking up the station into smaller modules for safe disposal.

The ISS has served as a testbed for technologies and international cooperation in space. Lessons from long-duration spaceflight—such as closed-loop life support, modular infrastructure, and remote operations—inform planning for future deep space missions to the Moon, Mars, and beyond. As interest in deep space exploration increases, the station’s legacy continues in its contributions to engineering, science, and humanity’s capacity to live and work in space.
